    Pollution Notes for Responsible Divers

    01 UV Filters

    How they enter: Some sunscreen ingredients wash off the skin while swimming or showering.

    Why they matter: Certain chemical UV filters may affect coral, algae and other aquatic organisms.

    Dive action: Wear UV-protective clothing and choose sunscreen carefully. NOAA

    02 Microplastics

    How they enter: Synthetic swimwear, ropes and diving equipment may shed tiny plastic fibres through wear and friction.

    Why they matter: Microplastics persist in the ocean and can be swallowed by marine life.

    Dive action: Maintain equipment, avoid disposable plastics and remove loose litter safely. UNEP

    03 PFAS

    How they enter: PFAS may wash or leach from products containing water-, oil- or stain-resistant treatments. Divers are only one possible source.

    Why they matter: These chemicals are highly persistent and can remain in water and living organisms.

    Dive action: Choose PFAS-free products where clearly verified and replace equipment only when necessary. US EPA

    04 Endocrine-Disrupting Chemicals

    How they enter: Some personal-care products and equipment chemicals may wash into the sea during use.

    Why they matter: Certain substances can interfere with hormones, development or reproduction in wildlife.

    Dive action: Use fewer unnecessary products and never rinse chemical-covered equipment directly into natural water. OECD

    Important: These categories can overlap. Some UV filters and PFAS may also have endocrine-disrupting effects; they are not always four completely separate pollutant groups.

    Science Guide

    Ocean Science Guide

    Read the ocean through the signals that shape reef health, from water chemistry to invisible pollutants.

    Ocean Health Indicators

    Potential Temperature

    Potential temperature reflects the ocean's heat content and helps track warming caused by excess human-made heat.

    Key signal: ocean heat storage

    Water Velocity

    Current speed controls how heat, salt, nutrients and pollutants move after they enter the sea.

    Use: track pollutant pathways

    Seawater pH

    pH shows ocean acidity. More CO2 forms carbonic acid, weakening coral skeletons and shell formation.

    Normal range: pH 7.5-8.2

    Dissolved Oxygen

    Fish and benthic life depend on dissolved oxygen. Low levels create hypoxia stress, often after algal blooms.

    Healthy coastal water: 6-8 mg/L

    Phosphate

    Phosphate feeds phytoplankton, but excess runoff and wastewater can trigger eutrophication and algal blooms.

    Typical seawater: below 0.1 mmol/m3

    Chlorophyll-a

    Chlorophyll-a indicates phytoplankton biomass, ocean productivity and possible eutrophication when unusually high.

    Signal: phytoplankton biomass

    What Are They?

    Unlike traditional pollutants such as oil spills or heavy metals, emerging pollutants are difficult to see and harder to trace. Their risk comes from low-dose, long-term exposure.

    • Invisible presence: Extremely low concentrations, from ng/L to ug/L, can still create significant effects.
    • Persistent residues: Some, like PFAS, barely degrade and are known as forever chemicals.
    • Mixture effects: Multiple pollutants can combine into toxicity that is greater than a single substance.
    • Diver pathways: Dive gear, sunscreen and personal care products can carry pollutants directly into the ocean.

    How Widespread?

    • PFAS have been detected in marine life from coastal fish to polar seals.
    • Microplastics have reached remote environments, including Antarctic sea ice and the Mariana Trench.
    • EDCs pose high risks at 65% of global sampling sites.
    • UV filters show elevated concentrations in tourist areas linked to diving and swimming.
    • China's Bohai, Yellow, East China and South China Seas show multiple emerging pollutants, with higher levels near intensive economic activity and dense populations.

    Why Your Actions Matter

    • 25-40% of chemical sunscreen can wash off within 20 minutes of entering the water.
    • PFAS coatings on dive gear can release chemicals as equipment is used and ages.
    • Personal care products containing EDCs can reach the ocean through shower wastewater.
    • Gear wear and tear can shed microplastics directly into the water.

    Pollutant File 01

    PFAS

    Forever chemicals - your dive suit's coating outlasts you in the sea.

    Half-life >41 years

    PFAS barely degrade, leaving persistent residues in marine environments.

    • Detected in marine life from coastal fish to polar seals.
    • Dive gear coatings can release chemicals through use and aging.
    • Choose durable gear carefully, maintain it longer, and avoid unnecessary coated replacements.

    Pollutant File 02

    Microplastics

    Invisible plastic dust - from your fins to Antarctic ice.

    170 trillion particles

    Microplastics can travel through currents and settle far from where they were released.

    • Detected in remote environments including Antarctic sea ice and the Mariana Trench.
    • Gear wear and tear can shed small fragments directly into the water.
    • Rinse, repair and retire gear responsibly before it begins shedding heavily.
    Return

    Pollutant File 03

    EDCs

    Hormone hijackers - fish change sex, coral larvae cannot settle.

    High risk at 65% of global sites

    Endocrine-disrupting chemicals can interfere with biological signaling even at low concentrations.

    • EDCs pose high risks at 65% of global sampling sites.
    • Personal care products can enter the ocean through shower wastewater.
    • Reduce unnecessary products before coastal activity and rinse responsibly away from direct marine discharge.
    Return

    Pollutant File 04

    UV Filters

    Sunscreen's hidden toll - what you apply, the reef absorbs.

    25-40% washes off in 20 minutes

    UV filters show elevated concentrations in tourist areas linked to diving and swimming.

    • Chemical sunscreen can wash off quickly after entering the water.
    • Reefs receive that residue directly during coastal recreation.
    • Use physical protection first, apply products carefully, and avoid entering water immediately after application.
    Return

    Pollutant File 05

    Antibiotics

    Aquaculture's invisible residue - fueling superbugs.

    0.12-309.71 ng/L in seawater

    Antibiotic residues can remain in coastal waters and contribute to resistance pressure.

    • Some seawater samples show detectable antibiotic concentrations.
    • Residues are often invisible during a dive, even when present.
    • Responsible seafood choices and coastal wastewater awareness help reduce upstream pressure.
    Return

    Pollutant File 06

    Micro-nanoplastics

    Small enough to cross cell membranes - from seawater to your blood.

    Detected in Antarctic sea ice

    Micro-nanoplastics are small enough to move through biological boundaries and remote ocean systems.

    • They have been detected in Antarctic sea ice.
    • Their scale makes them difficult to see, filter and remove.
    • Reduce disposable plastics near water and keep aging synthetic gear from shedding into the sea.

    About BlueTrace

    Every dive leaves a trace.

    The question is what kind of trace we choose to leave behind.

    Our Story

    The idea behind BlueTrace began beneath the ocean's surface.

    During our diving experiences, we witnessed plastic waste drifting through the water and coral reefs showing signs of stress and degradation. We realized that while many divers deeply care about the ocean, few understand how everyday choices, from sunscreen use to equipment habits, can leave invisible traces on marine ecosystems.

    This experience inspired us to create BlueTrace, a platform that transforms underwater observations and scientific knowledge into an interactive learning journey.

    Who We Are

    BlueTrace is a student-led ocean awareness project developed by a team of high school researchers, designers and divers united by a passion to understand and protect marine ecosystems.

    Our Mission

    To empower divers with scientific and practical knowledge for more responsible ocean exploration.

    Through interactive experiences, we aim to help ocean lovers understand their impact and make choices that better protect the ecosystems they admire.

    Every dive leaves a trace. The question is what kind of trace we choose to leave behind.

    Our Approach

    Built within 24 hours using Vibe Coding, BlueTrace combines diver observations, environmental research, AI-assisted development, and interactive design to transform marine science into an immersive experience.

    By connecting human choices with visible changes in coral ecosystems, we turn complex environmental issues into a story that divers can see, understand, and act upon.

    Our Vision

    We envision a future where responsible diving becomes part of ocean exploration, where every diver is not only an observer, but also a protector of the marine environments they love.
